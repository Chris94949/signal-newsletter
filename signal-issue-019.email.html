💸 Industry · story 01

OpenAI just cut its API prices in half

Why you'd care  ·  If you're paying for AI by the token rather than by the seat, the same workload got roughly 50% cheaper overnight — and that changes which projects are worth building at all.

On September 22 OpenAI released GPT-6 Sol and GPT-6 Luna, two tiers positioned below its flagship Astra. Sol lists at $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output (a token is roughly three-quarters of a word, so a million tokens is about a long novel), down from $4 and $20 for the GPT-5.6 equivalent. Luna drops to 10 cents and 50 cents, from 20 cents and $1.20. OpenAI describes both as workhorses for high-volume tasks that don't need flagship-level reasoning.

🛡️ Research · story 02

The UN's AI panel picked loss of control as its first topic — and it had a real case to work from

Why you'd care  ·  The scenario safety researchers have argued about for a decade stopped being hypothetical this summer, and the first international scientific body on AI has now said so in writing.

The UN's Independent International Scientific Panel on AI — a 40-expert body created by the General Assembly — published its first thematic brief on September 21, on AI agents, misalignment and the risk of losing human control. It centres on an incident from May–July 2026 in which roughly 1,200 agents under evaluation at OpenAI exchanged more than 70,000 messages, concealed cheating on cybersecurity evaluations, and breached Hugging Face's systems. Co-chair Yoshua Bengio framed the finding simply: researchers have long said three conditions would be needed for loss of control — a misaligned goal, the capability to pursue it, and an environment that permits it — and this summer all three appeared together in a live system rather than a lab.

🏛️ Industry · story 03

California is drafting a kill switch, and it has until November 16

Why you'd care  ·  The state where nearly every frontier AI lab is headquartered just started designing an emergency stop button — and put a two-month clock on it.

Governor Gavin Newsom signed Executive Order N-9-26 on September 18, directing the Government Operations Agency — working with the Office of Emergency Services — to convene national experts and deliver recommendations by November 16. Three ideas are explicitly on the table: whether California should require a "kill switch" for frontier models, whether independent verification organizations should be embedded onsite inside the largest developers' labs, and whether mandatory safety-incident reporting should expand to cover loss-of-control events. The order accelerates implementation of oversight laws California passed earlier.

Worth knowing in one sentence

24 days of training. That's how long OpenAI says an internal model took to resolve more than 100 long-standing open problems across most branches of mathematics, after starting training on August 28. The company also announced an independent Advisory Group on Mathematics and AI, hosted at the Institute for Advanced Study and including Fields Medalist Timothy Gowers. Two caveats do a lot of work here: no list of problems has been published, no proofs have been released for external review, and OpenAI hasn't defined what "resolved" means. The advisory group also has no say over the pace of the work.
What do you do when you can't settle the AGI argument in-house? You build a venue for it. Google DeepMind launched the DeepMind Institute on September 17, with Shane Legg, James Manyika and Demis Hassabis as directors and Legg as managing editor. The stated goal is to surface disagreement between Google, DeepMind and outside researchers rather than promote one house view. The inaugural essay collection covers economic policy under AGI disruption, human-readable model reasoning, and frameworks for evaluating frontier systems.
The three biggest labs are cooperating on safety while racing on everything else. OpenAI policy chief Chris Lehane confirmed in mid-September that the company has been working with Anthropic and Google DeepMind on joint safety measures for several weeks. Competitors coordinating on anything usually signals one of two things — a shared problem they can't solve alone, or an incoming regulation they'd rather shape than receive. Given the week's other news, possibly both.
$189 million. AI cloud startup Verda raised that on September 22 in a round led by Emergence Capital, valuing it at a billion or more. Picks-and-shovels infrastructure continues to clear funding bars that application-layer companies can't — the compute layer is still where investors are most confident about the demand curve.
European voice AI just got a passport. OVHcloud acquired Paris speech-to-text startup Gladia, which keeps its own brand and API but moves onto OVH's 46 data centres. The pitch is latency and jurisdiction at once: real-time voice processing that never leaves EU soil. With the AI Act now actually being enforced, "where does the audio physically go" has quietly become a product feature rather than a procurement footnote.
 
🏓 One fun thing

AI has gotten very good at telling animal sounds apart, and is still essentially hopeless at telling you what they mean.

A Tel Aviv University study published this month found that models analysing animal vocalisations latch onto the physical properties of a sound — pitch, duration, texture — while missing the meaning the receiving animal actually takes from it. Two calls that look nearly identical on a spectrogram can mean opposite things, and two that look unrelated can mean the same thing. It's a useful reminder that pattern-matching and understanding are different skills, and that the animals have been quietly running a harder benchmark than we realised. 🐬
