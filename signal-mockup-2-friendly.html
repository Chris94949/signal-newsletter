🗞️ This week's three stories
🤖 Frontier · story 01

China's 12-day open-coding blitz

In a 12-day window in mid-May, four Chinese labs — Z.ai (GLM-5.1), MiniMax (M2.7), Moonshot (Kimi K2.6), and DeepSeek (V4) — all released open-weights coding models that hit the same agentic-engineering ceiling as frontier closed models, at roughly a third of the inference cost. Quietly, in 12 days.

Why this matters
The frontier isn't moving up anymore — it's moving down on price. When open models hit the same ceiling at a third the cost, "which model is smartest?" becomes a less interesting question than "where does the money go when intelligence is cheap?" Expect inference-cost wars, more code agents in product, and a fresh round of build-vs-buy at every enterprise.
🌐 Industry · story 02

Gemini stops being a chatbot

At Google I/O on May 19, Google announced Gemini 4.0 plus three companions: Gemini Omni (a video model whose outputs are grounded in real knowledge), Gemini Spark (a personal agent that runs tasks across Workspace, Search, Chrome, YouTube), and Gemini 3.5 Flash hitting general availability — frontier intelligence at ~4× the speed. TechCrunch ↗

Why this matters
The shift isn't any one model — it's that Gemini is being repositioned from a destination app to an OS-layer across Google's surfaces. The chatbot-vs-chatbot framing already feels dated; the new question is which AI lives quietly inside the tools you already use.
🛡️ Security · story 03

The first AI security crossover

Palo Alto Networks' May Patch Wednesday disclosed 26 CVEs — over 5× their typical monthly volume — and most were surfaced by frontier AI models scanning code, not human researchers. Palo Alto ↗

Why this matters
This is the crossover month: AI's contribution to defensive security stopped being a future story and became a production reality. The flip side is uncomfortable — the same class of models is finding the same bugs for attackers. Expect a year of triage catch-up, faster patch cycles, and awkward disclosure conversations.

🏓 One fun thing

Sony AI's "Ace" is the first known autonomous system to compete with pro-level humans in a popular physical sport — quietly clearing a benchmark researchers have circled for two decades. The wild part: it learned mostly from self-play.
