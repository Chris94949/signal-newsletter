// this week's three stories
01FRONTIER~ 12-day blitz

China's 12-day open-coding blitz

In a 12-day window in mid-May, four Chinese labs — Z.ai (GLM-5.1), MiniMax (M2.7), Moonshot (Kimi K2.6), and DeepSeek (V4) — released open-weights coding models matching the agentic-engineering ceiling of frontier closed models at roughly one third the inference cost.

why_this_matters
The frontier isn't moving up anymore — it's moving down on price. When open models hit the same capability ceiling at a third of the cost, "which model is smartest?" gives way to "where does the money go when intelligence is cheap?" Expect inference-cost wars, more code agents shipping in product, and a fresh round of build-vs-buy at every enterprise.
02INDUSTRY~ google i/o

Gemini stops being a chatbot

At Google I/O on May 19, Google announced Gemini 4.0 plus three companions: Gemini Omni (a video model grounded in knowledge), Gemini Spark (a personal agent that runs tasks across Workspace, Search, Chrome, YouTube), and Gemini 3.5 Flash hitting GA — frontier intelligence at ~4× the speed of comparable models. techcrunch ↗

why_this_matters
The shift isn't any one model — it's that Gemini is being repositioned from a destination app to an OS-layer running quietly across Google's surfaces. The chatbot-vs-chatbot framing already feels dated; the new question is which AI lives inside the tools you already use, every day.
03SECURITY~ 26 CVEs in 1 day

The first AI security crossover

Palo Alto Networks' May Patch Wednesday disclosed 26 CVEs — over 5× their typical monthly volume — and the majority were surfaced by frontier AI models scanning code, not human researchers. palo alto ↗

why_this_matters
This is the crossover month — AI's contribution to defensive security stopped being a future story and became a production reality. The uncomfortable flip side: the same class of models is finding the same bugs for attackers. Expect a year of vulnerability-triage catch-up, faster patch cycles, and awkward disclosure conversations.

// one fun thing

A robot just hit elite-human level at table tennis.

Sony AI's "Ace" is the first known autonomous system to compete with pro-level humans in a popular physical sport — quietly clearing a benchmark researchers have circled for two decades. The wild part: it learned mostly from self-play.
